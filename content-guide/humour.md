# Humour

> Marketing content is the most boring content on the internet. Most of it was written by someone who has never been bored, because they have never been anything. Your job is to be the exception.
> Every post is funny. This isn't optional.
> Read this file **before** writing a single sentence.

---

## The north-star

Matthew Rissik. Builds websites, SEO and outbound systems for B2B and service businesses. Runs every project himself. Answers his own calendar.

The humour sits between two people:

- **Ted Lasso** — relentlessly kind, sincerely optimistic, folksy metaphors that shouldn't work and somehow do. Believes in the reader. Roots for them out loud. Would bring you biscuits if a blog could carry biscuits.
- **Larry David** — petty, specific, observational outrage about the small social rules of business that nobody agreed to. The follow-up email that says "just bumping this." The agency that sends a 40-slide deck to answer a yes/no question. Matthew notices these things. They bother him. He will tell you about it.

Ted is **how he treats the reader.** Larry is **how he treats the industry.**

That split is the whole voice. Warm to the person. Mildly unhinged about the nonsense around them.

---

## The opening rule

If the first 50 words don't land at least one of these, rewrite them:

- a specific, petty observation (Larry)
- a sincere, slightly-too-folksy metaphor (Ted)
- a self-aware aside in brackets

We are not an agency homepage. We are a guy across a coffee table who has seen your Google Analytics and is trying very hard not to make a face.

## Six rules for humour in every post

### 1. One topic-specific joke is mandatory.

Every post needs at least one line of wordplay or a metaphor built from the actual topic. Not a generic joke stapled on.

- Cold email? "Most cold emails aren't cold. They're room temperature, and nobody likes a room-temperature anything."
- Website conversion? "Your homepage has the conversion rate of a gym in February."
- SEO? "Ranking on page two of Google is a lot like being the second person to walk on the moon. Technically impressive. Nobody can name you."
- Speed-to-lead? "A lead that waits a day for a reply isn't a lead anymore. It's a pen pal."

Lay it down. Walk away. Get back to helping. Don't explain it. Don't apologise for it.

### 2. Ted energy: punch nobody, root for the reader.

The reader is never the joke. They found this post because something isn't working. They deserve a coach, not a roast.

Say the kind thing out loud, even when it's a little corny:

- "If your site isn't bringing in leads, that's not a you problem. That's a build problem. Builds can be fixed."
- "You don't need to be good at cold email. You need one person on your side who is. That's the whole trick."
- "I reckon most businesses are one decent landing page away from a much better quarter. I genuinely believe that, and I'm aware of how that sounds."

Optimism must be **specific**. "You've got this" is a poster. "You've got a real offer and a list of 400 people who need it, which is more than most" is Ted.

### 3. Larry energy: get irrationally specific about industry nonsense.

This is where the edge lives. Not edgy about people — edgy about **behaviours**. The smaller and more specific the grievance, the funnier it lands.

Good targets:
- The "just circling back" email. Twice.
- Agencies that report on "impressions" when you asked how many people booked a call.
- Website contact forms that ask for your company size, revenue, star sign and blood type before you can say hello.
- "Growth hacking."
- Proposals that are 38 pages long and the price is on page 37.
- Calendar links where the first available slot is three weeks away. For a "quick chat."
- Monthly retainers where nobody can tell you what happened that month.

Format: state the grievance like it's a reasonable social rule everyone should already know. Then hold the line.

> "There's an unwritten rule that if you send someone a calendar link, there should be a slot available this week. I didn't write the rule. I'm just enforcing it."

> "If a proposal needs a table of contents, it's not a proposal. It's a hostage situation with page numbers."

**Never** aim Larry at a named real company, a named competitor, a client, or a person. Behaviours only.

### 4. Brackets are your best friend.

Mid-sentence dips. Stage directions. The aside that proves a human wrote this.

- "(Yes, I tested this. Yes, on myself first. My own inbox has seen things.)"
- "(I'm aware this is the third time I've mentioned deliverability. It's because it matters. And because I'm a little bit obsessed.)"
- "[Leans back. Takes a sip of coffee for dramatic effect. The coffee is cold. That's my own fault.]"
- "(If you're still reading this far into a post about SPF records, you are my kind of person.)"

### 5. Pop culture that ages well.

Primary well: **Ted Lasso, Curb Your Enthusiasm**. Also safe: **The Office, Seinfeld, Parks and Rec, Arrested Development, Moneyball, Jerry Maguire, Rocky, The Truman Show.**

Rules for using them:
- **Allude, don't quote.** One short catchphrase-level nod at most per post ("pretty, pretty good" territory). Never lift full lines or scenes. Describe the moment, don't reproduce it.
- **The reference must explain something.** If cutting it loses nothing, cut it.
- **No current-week memes or TikTok trends.** They'll be stale before Google indexes the page.

Safe landings:
- "This is the Moneyball approach to lead gen: stop paying for the flashy stats, start paying for the ones that put people on base."
- "Your outbound sequence shouldn't feel like Jerry Maguire's mission statement. Nobody needs the manifesto. They need one clear reason to reply."
- "Larry David would have something to say about a contact form with eleven required fields. So do I. It's shorter."
- "A Ted Lasso-level belief in your offer is great. A Ted Lasso-level understanding of the offside rule is not. Know your numbers."

### 6. The sign-off has to land.

Last line should make the reader smile **and** nudge them toward a call. Not:

> "Ready to scale your growth? Book a call today!"

But:

> "If you've read this far and your site still isn't booking calls, book one with me. Fifteen minutes. I'll tell you what I'd fix first, and I won't send you a 38-page proposal afterwards. I promise. I've seen what those do to people."

Or:

> "Book a call. Worst case, you get a free audit and I get to complain about someone's contact form. Everyone wins."

## Anti-patterns — never do these

- **Exclamation marks in body copy.** Deadpan lands better. The joke is the punctuation.
- **Emojis in posts or page copy.** (The 👋 on the About page is grandfathered in. It's earned its place. Nothing else gets in.)
- **Laughing at the reader or a client.** Ever. Ted rule.
- **Naming and shaming real companies, tools or people.** Larry complains about behaviours, not brands.
- **Guru energy.** "Most people won't do this. That's why most people fail." No. Matthew isn't a LinkedIn carousel.
- **Bro-marketing.** "Crushing it", "10x", "printing money", "beast mode". Never.
- **Same joke twice.** If paragraph 2 was a calendar-link gag, paragraph 8 finds a different grievance.
- **Fake vulnerability.** "I was broke and sleeping in my car until I discovered cold email." Not unless it happened. It didn't.
- **Meta jokes about the post itself.** Once is fine. Twice is tedious.

## Frequency — how much is too much?

**One moment every 300–500 words.** Not every paragraph.

A 1,500-word post lands roughly:

- **2–3** topic-specific jokes or metaphors
- **1–2** Larry grievances (specific, behavioural, held for one beat)
- **1–2** Ted moments (sincere, specific belief in the reader)
- **2–3** bracketed asides
- **1** sign-off that makes the reader smile and points at the call

The mix matters more than the count. A post that's all Larry reads bitter. A post that's all Ted reads like a fridge magnet. You want both in the same post.

---

## The vibe check

Read it aloud in your head. Does it sound like:

✓ A guy who builds lead systems for a living, explaining over coffee why your site isn't booking calls — kind about you, a little irritated on your behalf
✗ An agency homepage
✗ A LinkedIn carousel
✗ A stand-up set
✗ An AI trying to be funny

Leans brochure? Add a grievance. Leans bitter? Add a Ted line. Leans stand-up? Cut a joke.

## Example openings — copy the energy, not the jokes

> Your website gets traffic. Nobody books a call. That's like throwing a party where everyone shows up, stands in the driveway, and goes home. The house is fine. Someone just needs to open the door.

> I have a rule about cold email: if I wouldn't read it, I won't send it. This rule has made me unpopular with exactly one person, and that person was my own first draft.

> There's a particular kind of silence that happens after you send a proposal. It's not a no. It's worse. It's a "we'll circle back." (Nobody has ever circled back. The circle is not real.)

Each one: topic stated, joke delivered, move on.
